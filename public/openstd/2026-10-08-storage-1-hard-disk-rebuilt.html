① The hard disk, rebuilt: why moving metal sets the rules

Chapters 44 and 43 both answer the hard disk. Chapter 44 asks what changes when nothing moves; chapter 43 asks how a file system should write when moving the arm is the expensive part. Both lean on two facts rebuilt here: a small random access is slow because metal must move first, and the OS sees only a numbered array of sectors, each fixed to one spot.

Section tags: ★ core = read every line · main = read · skim = the figure and the CARRY line at the end of the section are enough.

§1

What is inside a hard disk, and what has to move?

main · read

Figure 1. A 2.5-inch laptop hard disk with the lid off. The arm is resting on its parking ramp, so the head is off the platter. Photo: Evan-Amos, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0; labels added.

Figure 2. Where the tracks and sectors are; a photo cannot show them, because they are magnetic. Left: one surface from above, not to scale: the teal ring is one track, and the amber piece of it is one sector. Right: the same drive from the side. Each platter has a top and a bottom surface, and each surface has its own head. Bottom: the teal track cut open into a row of 512-byte sectors; the violet bracket is one 4 KB request, eight of them in a row.

A hard disk stores bits as tiny magnetized regions on a platter, a rigid disc coated with a thin magnetic layer (Figure 1). Both surfaces of a platter hold data. One or more platters are clamped to the spindle, a motor hub that turns them together at a constant rate, typically 7,200 to 15,000 rotations per minute (RPM). The rate never changes to suit a request: the platter keeps turning, and the drive waits for the data to come round. OSTEP 37.2

Data on a surface lies in concentric rings called tracks, and each track is cut into sectors of 512 bytes (Figure 2, ① ② ⑤). The sector is the smallest unit the drive reads or writes. A request is usually bigger: file systems read and write 4 KB at a time, which is eight sectors in a row (Figure 2, ⑤, violet). OSTEP's model uses 512-byte sectors, as this page does; drives made since about 2011 store 4,096-byte sectors physically (“Advanced Format”), and many still show the OS 512-byte ones. Tracks are packed so tightly that hundreds of them fit into the width of a human hair. OSTEP 37.1–37.2

A head reads by sensing the magnetization that passes under it and writes by changing it. There is one head per surface, and a surface is one side of a platter, so every platter has two heads, one above it and one below (Figure 2, ④). The heads ride on the arm, which an actuator, a voice-coil motor at the arm's pivot, swings across the surface (Figure 3). OSTEP 37.2

Figure 3. The arm of a 3.5-inch drive over its platter. A seek is the arm turning about the pivot until the head sits over the wanted track. Photo: Matthew Field, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0; labels added.

So two things move. The platter turns under the head, and the arm swings across the platter. Before a single byte can be read, the arm has to reach the right track and the right sector has to rotate under the head. That waiting is where the time goes, and §2 measures it.

CARRYA hard disk is a spinning platter and a swinging arm. Every request waits for the two to line up before any byte moves.

§2

Where do the milliseconds of one request go?

★ core · read every line

Figure 4. Left: a disk with three tracks, the rings outer 0–11, middle 12–23 and inner 24–35, each cut into 12 sectors, the numbered pieces (OSTEP Figure 37.3, redrawn), serving "read sector 11". Right: the same three costs as time bars, for one random 4 KB read and for one 100 MB sequential read. Amber is time spent positioning; teal is time spent moving bytes.

Figure 4 follows one request, "read sector 11", through OSTEP's toy disk: three tracks of twelve sectors, with the head starting over the inner track. Three things happen, in a fixed order.

① Seek. The arm swings from the inner track to the outer one. A seek is four motions, not one: the arm accelerates, coasts at full speed, decelerates, and finally settles, holding until the head is locked onto the track. Settling alone takes 0.5–2 ms, because the drive must be certain it is on the right track, not merely close to it. Real drives quote average seeks of 4–9 ms. OSTEP 37.3

② Rotation. The head is over the right track, but sector 11 is somewhere else on the ring, so the head waits for the platter to carry it round. On average that is half a rotation. At 15,000 RPM the platter turns 250 times a second, so one rotation takes 4 ms and the average wait is 2 ms. At 7,200 RPM one rotation takes 8.3 ms and the average wait is about 4.2 ms. OSTEP 37.3–37.4

③ Transfer. Sector 11 passes under the head and its bytes are read. A 4 KB request is eight consecutive sectors, normally side by side on one track, so after one seek and one rotation all eight pass under the head in a single pass. This is the only step that moves data, and it is quick: at 125 MB/s a 4 KB read takes 4,096 ÷ 125,000,000 s ≈ 30 µs. OSTEP 37.4

The three add up to the time of one I/O, and the rate a workload sees is its size divided by that time:

Where Figure 4’s numbers come from. ① The rate is one track’s bytes times rotations per second. ② Outer tracks hold more sectors, so 125 MB/s is the top speed. ③ The random read to scale: seek and rotation fill the bar, transfer is a 3-pixel sliver. ④ The rate belongs to one head, whatever the number of surfaces.

The 125 MB/s in the table is not a separate measurement; it follows from the geometry (Figure 5). ① While the head sits over one track, one rotation carries every sector of that track under it once, so the peak rate is the bytes on one track times the rotations per second. The Cheetah turns 15,000 times a minute, 250 times a second, and 125 MB/s ÷ 250 = 0.5 MB: about 1,000 sectors of 512 bytes on an outer track. The Barracuda turns 120 times a second at 105 MB/s, so its tracks hold about 0.875 MB. ② Tracks near the edge are longer than tracks near the spindle, so drives give them more sectors; a band of neighbouring tracks with the same sector count is a zone. Every track turns at the same RPM, so an outer track delivers more bytes per second, and the spec's 125 MB/s is the outer-zone speed. Inner zones deliver roughly half on typical drives. OSTEP 37.3 (multi-zoned drives), Figure 37.5

So a random 4 KB read costs about 6 ms, of which almost 6 ms is waiting and 0.03 ms is work: for more than 99% of the request, nothing is being read. The rate comes out at 4 KB ÷ 6 ms ≈ 0.66 MB/s, on a drive whose head can move 125 MB/s. ③ Figure 5 draws this read to scale. The arithmetic stays short with one unit trick: KB per ms equals MB per s, because the top and the bottom are both scaled by a thousand. So 4 KB ÷ 6 ms = 0.66 KB/ms = 0.66 MB/s, and the transfer is 4 KB ÷ 125 KB/ms = 0.032 ms.

Now read 100 MB that lies in one contiguous run (Figure 4, lower bars; Figure 5, ③, last line). The drive pays one seek and one rotation, about 6 ms, and then streams for 100 MB ÷ 125 MB/s = 800 ms. The rate is 100 MB ÷ 0.806 s ≈ 124 MB/s, almost the peak. Same drive, same speed while the bytes flow, and yet the sequential read runs about 125 ÷ 0.66 ≈ 190 times faster than the random one. The Barracuda, a cheaper 7,200 RPM drive with a 9 ms average seek, does worse: about 13.2 ms per random 4 KB read (0.31 MB/s) against 105 MB/s sequential, a gap of about 340×. OSTEP 37.4, Figures 37.5–37.6

④ All of these rates belong to one head. The Cheetah and the Barracuda each have 4 platters, so 8 surfaces and 8 heads, but the heads hang on one arm, and the drive reads through one of them at a time: the tracks of different surfaces never line up closely enough for every head to sit on its own track at once. More platters therefore add capacity, not speed. Per surface, the Cheetah holds 300 GB ÷ 8 = 37.5 GB and the Barracuda 1 TB ÷ 8 = 125 GB; drives of the 2020s hold about 2–3 TB per platter. The exception is the dual-actuator drive (for example Seagate's MACH.2), which gives two groups of heads their own arms and so reads through two at once. OSTEP Figure 37.5

The gap also widens over the years. Packing bits more densely raises the transfer rate, since more bits pass under the head per rotation, but seek and rotation improve slowly, because small and cheap motors are hard to make faster. The log-structured file system of page ④ was designed in the early 1990s around exactly this trend. OSTEP 43, introduction

CARRYTI/O = Tseek + Trotation + Ttransfer. For a small random request the first two are over 99% of the time, so the same disk runs 190–340× faster on one long contiguous run. OSTEP's rule: use disks sequentially; when that is impossible, transfer in large chunks. The peak rate is one track per rotation, through one head.

§3

What does the OS see, and what is it promised?

★ core · read every line

Figure 6. The interface and the place. Left: the OS sees n sectors numbered 0 to n−1. Right: on a hard disk each number is one fixed spot, so a rewrite goes back to the same spot. Page ② draws the SSD version of this figure, where the array stays and the fixed spot goes.

The OS never sees platters or tracks. The drive presents itself as an array of n sectors numbered 0 to n−1 (Figure 6, left), and this number is called the logical block address (LBA). The OS issues requests such as "read sector 7" or "write 8 sectors starting at 1,000", and the drive turns each number into a surface, a track and an angle. OSTEP 37.1

The interface makes one hard promise: a write of a single 512-byte sector is atomic, meaning it lands completely or not at all. File systems usually write 4 KB at a time, which is eight sectors, and for those there is no promise. If power fails partway, only some of the eight may land; this is a torn write. The crash-recovery machinery of file systems, including the checkpoint protocol of LFS on page ④, exists because of this gap. OSTEP 37.1

Clients also rely on two promises the interface never states, which Schlosser and Ganger called the drive's unwritten contract: two blocks with nearby numbers are faster to access together than two far apart, and a contiguous run is the fastest access of all. §2 is why both hold. File systems are built on this contract; page ③ shows that the SSD has a contract of its own, with different terms. OSTEP 37.1

On a hard disk the mapping from number to place is static: sector 5 lies on one surface, one track, at one angle, from the day the drive is made (Figure 6, right). Writing sector 5 means waiting for that spot and magnetizing it again. There is no separate erase step, rewriting does not wear the spot out in any way the OS must manage, and the drive keeps no table of where data went, because nothing ever goes anywhere. OSTEP uses exactly this point when it introduces the SSD's trim command: For a standard hard drive, trim isn't particularly useful, because the drive has a static mapping of block addresses to specific platter, track, and sector(s). OSTEP 44.8, aside on trim

This property, overwrite in place with address equal to place, is the one flash cannot keep. A flash chip cannot overwrite a page, so the SSD keeps the array of Figure 6 but gives up the fixed spot, and nearly everything in chapter 44 follows from that single change.

CARRYThe OS sees an array of numbered sectors. Only a single-sector write is atomic. On a hard disk each number is a fixed spot, so a rewrite simply overwrites that spot: no erase, no wear, no map.
